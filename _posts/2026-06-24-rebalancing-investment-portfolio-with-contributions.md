When I started paying closer attention to my portfolio, I assumed rebalancing meant selling whatever had grown above its target and buying what had fallen behind. That is one way to restore a target allocation, but it is not the only way.

If you are still contributing regularly, you can often move the portfolio gradually by directing new money toward the assets that are below their target weights. This approach can reduce unnecessary transactions, but it takes time and does not make an unsuitable portfolio suitable.

> **Educational note:** This article describes a general portfolio-management idea, not a recommendation to buy, sell, or hold any security. The right approach depends on your goals, risk tolerance, time horizon, taxes, costs, and local rules.

## Start with a target allocation

A target allocation is a plan for how your portfolio is divided among broad asset groups or investments. For example, someone might decide in advance how much to allocate to global equities, bonds, and a small diversifying position.

The target is useful only when it reflects a real plan. Before rebalancing, write down:

- What each holding is meant to do in the portfolio.
- The target weight for each holding or asset group.
- How often you will review the allocation.
- What would make you change the plan.
- How much cash you need outside the portfolio for near-term goals.

A target allocation is not a prediction of which asset will perform best next. It is a way to choose how much risk you are willing to take and keep that decision from changing with every market move.

## How contribution-based rebalancing works

Suppose an asset has a target weight of 10% but currently represents 5% of the portfolio. If you make a new contribution, you can direct some or all of it toward that underweight position instead of buying every holding in its target proportions.

Over time, the contribution changes the portfolio weights without requiring a sale. The portfolio may move closer to its targets while you keep the existing positions intact.

A simple process is:

1. Calculate current weights using the portfolio’s current market value.
2. Compare each weight with its target.
3. Direct the next contribution toward assets that are below target, within limits you chose in advance.
4. Review periodically rather than reacting to every price movement.
5. Reassess if your goals, time horizon, or ability to tolerate losses change.

This can be easier to follow than frequent selling. It also turns monthly investing into a deliberate part of portfolio maintenance.

## Why I prefer a gradual approach

Selling a position can create transaction costs, tax consequences, or emotional friction, depending on the account and jurisdiction. If I can make progress through future contributions, I can avoid making a rushed decision just because a percentage moved away from its target.

The key is that “I do not want to sell at a loss” should not become the entire investment thesis. The price you paid is already in the past. For each holding, ask whether you would choose it today at its current portfolio weight, given your goals and alternatives. If not, a sale may still be worth considering after accounting for taxes and costs.

Contribution-based rebalancing is a method for implementing a portfolio decision. It does not answer whether the portfolio itself is diversified, affordable, or appropriate for your goals.

## Set guardrails for concentrated or thematic positions

A narrow industry or commodity fund can behave very differently from a broad market fund. If you choose to hold one, decide what role it has and set a maximum allocation before it becomes a large part of the portfolio.

A position limit can keep a speculative idea from quietly turning into a core holding after a strong run—or inviting repeated purchases after a decline. The limit should come from your risk plan, not from a desire to recover a loss or reach a round number.

## Do not rebalance money you may need soon

A portfolio allocation should not override a near-term financial goal. If you may need money for a home purchase or another major expense, keep that goal’s cash separate from long-term investments and account for its timing before directing every contribution into the portfolio.

The right balance between investing and saving for a near-term goal depends on when you need the money and how much uncertainty you can accept. Reaching an ETF target is not automatically more important than preserving liquidity for a planned purchase.

## A practical checklist

Before your next contribution, ask:

- Is my target allocation still connected to my actual goals?
- Which holdings are meaningfully below target?
- Am I changing the plan because of a written rule or because of recent performance?
- Will this contribution reduce cash needed for a near-term goal?
- Does my broker’s automated allocation feature buy only the assets I intend to reinforce?

Rebalancing with contributions can be a calm, repeatable way to maintain a portfolio. The benefit comes from following a considered plan—not from hitting exact percentages as quickly as possible.
