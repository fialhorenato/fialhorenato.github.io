When I first started comparing ETFs, it was easy to think that adding another fund automatically made a portfolio more diversified. But two funds with different names can own many of the same companies.

The number of funds is a poor shortcut for measuring diversification. What matters is what those funds hold, how much they hold, and how their risks relate to one another.

> **Educational note:** This article explains general portfolio concepts and is not a recommendation to buy or sell any fund. ETF holdings, benchmarks, fees, and tax treatment vary by product and jurisdiction.

## Look through the fund label

A broad global equity ETF may already include large companies from the United States. Adding a separate S&P 500 ETF can increase exposure to those same companies rather than adding an entirely new source of diversification.

That extra exposure may be intentional. The important thing is to know that it changes the portfolio. A second fund is not automatically a new asset class or a hedge against the first one.

For each fund, check:

- Its benchmark and investment universe.
- The largest underlying holdings and how concentrated they are.
- The split across countries, regions, sectors, and company sizes.
- Whether it holds stocks, bonds, commodities, or another type of asset.
- Its costs, currency exposure, and replication method.

The fund name and category are useful starting points, but the factsheet and holdings show what exposure you actually own.

## A simple overlap check

You do not need a perfect risk model to notice obvious overlap. Compare the largest holdings of each fund and ask whether the same companies appear repeatedly. Then compare their country and sector weights.

If you hold a global fund and a U.S. large-company fund, for example, examine how much additional U.S. exposure the second fund creates. If you hold several regional funds, check whether their combined weights still match the geographic allocation you intended.

The goal is not to eliminate every overlap. Overlap can be part of a deliberate decision. The goal is to distinguish deliberate concentration from accidental duplication.

## Diversification has several dimensions

A portfolio can contain many securities but still depend heavily on one country, sector, currency, or economic outcome. When reviewing diversification, consider:

- **Company concentration:** Are a few large holdings driving most of the exposure?
- **Geography:** How much depends on one country or region?
- **Sector:** Are several funds tilted toward the same industries?
- **Asset class:** Are all holdings exposed to equity-market risk?
- **Currency:** What currencies affect the value and spending goal?
- **Time horizon:** When might you need to sell or spend the money?

Different dimensions matter for different goals. Holding a global equity fund does not remove equity risk, and adding more equity funds may not protect against a broad decline in stocks.

## Decide whether extra exposure is intentional

Before adding a fund, write down what role it is meant to play. Is it intended to broaden the portfolio, increase a specific exposure, reduce volatility, or express a limited investment view?

Then consider whether the position size reflects that role. If a fund mostly repeats existing holdings, it may increase concentration without making the portfolio more robust. If that is the goal, make the choice deliberately and include it in your target allocation.

A useful review question is: **What new risk or exposure does this fund add to my current portfolio?** If the answer is unclear, pause and inspect the holdings before buying.

## Build from the whole portfolio

Evaluate funds together, not one at a time. A simple spreadsheet with each fund’s target weight and its major asset, region, sector, and company exposures can make duplication easier to see. Review it periodically rather than changing the portfolio in response to every market move.

Diversification is not about collecting tickers. It is about understanding which risks you own, how concentrated they are, and whether that mix still fits your plan.
