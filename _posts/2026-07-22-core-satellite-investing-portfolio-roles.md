A portfolio can contain broad equity funds, bonds, gold, and a narrow thematic investment—and still be hard to explain. One way I find useful to organize the discussion is to give each holding a job.

This is sometimes described as a core-and-satellite approach: a diversified core supports the main long-term plan, while smaller satellite positions express limited preferences or add exposures the investor understands. The labels do not make a portfolio safe or well-designed by themselves. They are a way to ask whether each position belongs and how large it should be.

> **Educational note:** This article describes a general framework, not a recommended allocation or a product endorsement. Asset behavior, taxation, fees, and investor protections vary by product and jurisdiction.

## Define the core around the goal

The core is the part of the portfolio intended to do most of the long-term work. For some investors, that may be a broad, low-cost fund. Others may use a combination of funds or instruments to reflect their time horizon, currency needs, and risk tolerance.

The important question is not whether a fund is labelled “core.” It is whether the overall core is diversified enough for the goal, affordable to hold, understandable, and something you can stick with through market declines.

A core should be assessed at the portfolio level. Several broad funds can overlap, while a single fund can still carry substantial market risk. Check what is inside and how the pieces fit together.

## Give the other holdings a clear role

Bonds, gold, and thematic funds are often added for different reasons, but none has one guaranteed behavior:

- **Bonds** may provide income or behave differently from equities, but bond prices can fall as interest rates, credit conditions, and inflation expectations change. Duration and credit quality matter.
- **Gold** may diversify some portfolios, but it does not produce interest or business earnings and its price can be volatile.
- **Thematic or sector funds** can provide focused exposure, but they may be concentrated, cyclical, or more volatile than a broad market fund.

These categories are not interchangeable. Before adding one, write what you expect it to do and what conditions could cause it not to do that job.

## Set a size limit before you need one

A narrow investment can grow into a large share of a portfolio after strong performance. A position limit helps keep a satellite from becoming the portfolio’s main risk by accident.

The limit should fit your finances and ability to tolerate losses. It should also be considered alongside existing exposure: a dedicated sector fund may add to a sector you already own through a broad index.

Decide in advance how you will respond if the position goes above or below the intended range. That rule can be implemented through contributions, rebalancing, or a review—not necessarily through an immediate trade.

## Avoid assigning a story after the fact

It is easy to describe a holding as “protection” after it has done well or as “long-term conviction” after it has fallen. A role is more useful when you define it before the outcome is known.

For each holding, note:

1. What risk or goal it addresses.
2. What evidence supports including it.
3. What could make it underperform or lose value.
4. How much of the portfolio it may represent.
5. When you will review whether it still belongs.

This does not remove uncertainty. It makes the uncertainty visible and gives you a reason to hold, resize, or remove the position beyond recent performance.

## Keep near-term money outside the long-term portfolio

A core-and-satellite framework is for organizing investments, not for deciding where every euro belongs. Money set aside for an emergency or a near-term purchase may need more stability and access than a long-term portfolio can provide.

Separate goals first, then decide how to allocate the money that is genuinely intended for investing. This helps avoid selling a volatile asset at an inconvenient time to cover a known expense.

## A portfolio-role review

For every holding, try to finish this sentence: **I own this because it ______, and I will review it if ______.**

If the sentence is difficult to complete, the position may be redundant, oversized, or based mainly on a recent story. A simpler portfolio with clear roles can be easier to understand and maintain than a longer list of funds.
